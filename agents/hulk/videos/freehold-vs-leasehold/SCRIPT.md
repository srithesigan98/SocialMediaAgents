# Narration (locked for TTS: Kokoro af_heart) — "Freehold vs leasehold"

Illustration used (made-up, labelled "Illustration" on screen): a leasehold unit with 35 years left on the lease.
Hedges are required ("often", "some", "may", "can", "unless") — don't strengthen them. No bank names, no
remaining-lease cut-off figure (each bank sets its own; guides quote 30, 40 or 60 years), no premium amounts,
no renewal promises (renewal is at the State Authority's discretion).

| id | beat | line | source |
|---|---|---|---|
| vo01 | hook | Same street, two condos. One is freehold, one is leasehold. What changes for you? | Framing only |
| vo02 | definitions | Freehold has no end date. Leasehold is a lease from the state, often for 99 years. | National Land Code: State land may be alienated in perpetuity or for a term; s.76 caps a term at 99 years; 99 is the most common term, 30/60 also exist (iProperty, StarProperty, list.my freehold-vs-leasehold guides) — hence "often" |
| vo03 | expiry | When the lease runs out, the land goes back to the state, unless it's renewed. | NLC s.46: alienated land reverts to and vests in the State Authority when the term on the title expires (law-firm / property-guide summaries, chialee.com.my, globallawexperts) |
| vo04 | renewal | Renewal isn't automatic. You apply before it ends, and pay a premium the state sets. | NLC s.90A (Peninsular) extension application before expiry; State Authority values the land, sets the premium (Form 5A) and may refuse (EdgeProp "Extend your land lease before it expires"; Low & Partners lease-extension note; iProperty renewal guide) |
| vo05 | by state | Each state has its own rules and rates, so the cost can differ a lot. | Land is a state matter (Federal Constitution); premium formulas and processes differ per state, e.g. Selangor's discretionary nominal-premium scheme (globallawexperts, EdgeProp, Malay Mail 2026-07 on PJ leasehold premiums) |
| vo06 | loan (illustration) | Illustration: 35 years left on the lease. Some banks may lend less, or for fewer years. | Illustration. No single BNM rule on remaining lease; lenders set their own cut-offs and may cut margin or tenure (StashAway MY, LoanStreet "most banks won't finance under 30 years", globallawexperts ~60 years) — hence "some" and "may" |
| vo07 | consent | Some titles also need state consent to sell or refinance. That can add weeks, or months. | A "restriction in interest" on the title (more common on leasehold) requires State Authority consent for a transfer or charge; processing quoted at 6-8 weeks up to months, longer in busy land offices (Mah Weng Kwai / Mondaq "Property transactions requiring state consent"; PropertyGenie; The Edge) — "some titles" because not every leasehold has the restriction |
| vo08 | upside | The upside: leasehold is often priced lower than a similar freehold nearby. | Guides put freehold at a premium over comparable leasehold, size varies and isn't guaranteed (StarProperty "price under the influence of perception"; list.my; ohmyhome) — hence "often", no % stated |
| vo09 | checklist | Before you buy, check the title: the tenure, the years left, and any restriction. | Tenure, expiry date and restrictions are endorsed on the title (iProperty land title guide) |
| vo10 | close | Comment "TITLE", and we'll check a unit's tenure and years left for you. | — |

Dropped in the skeptic pass: any remaining-lease bank cut-off figure (30 vs 40 vs 60 years — sources disagree),
"freehold rises faster in value" (one commentary says the two rise at a similar rate for 20-30 years),
a freehold price-premium % (15-25% figures are unsourced), "leasehold always needs consent" (Batu Caves example:
not all do; some freehold titles do), "renewal is usually approved for 99 years" (ministerial guidance, not law).
